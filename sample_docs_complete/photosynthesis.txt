Photosynthesis in Plants

Introduction

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae and some bacteria turn light energy into chemical energy stored in sugar. It happens mainly in the leaves, inside small structures called chloroplasts. Almost all life on Earth depends on photosynthesis, because it produces the oxygen we breathe and the food that starts most food chains.

Chlorophyll and Chloroplasts

Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, the green pigment that absorbs sunlight, mostly red and blue light. Green light is reflected, which is why leaves look green. Each chloroplast has stacks of flat membranes called thylakoids, and the fluid around them is called the stroma.

The Overall Equation

The overall equation is: carbon dioxide + water + light energy gives glucose + oxygen. In symbols, 6CO2 + 6H2O + light gives C6H12O6 + 6O2. Six molecules of carbon dioxide and six molecules of water produce one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen.

The Two Stages

There are two main stages. The light-dependent reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes. Here light energy splits water molecules, releasing oxygen and producing the energy carriers ATP and NADPH. The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma. It uses ATP and NADPH to turn carbon dioxide into glucose. The Calvin cycle does not need light directly, but it depends on the products of the light reactions.

How Plants Get the Raw Materials

Plants take in carbon dioxide through tiny pores in the leaves called stomata. Water is absorbed by the roots and travels up through the xylem. The oxygen produced is released into the air through the stomata. Guard cells open and close the stomata to control gas exchange and to reduce water loss.

What Happens to the Glucose

The plant uses some glucose immediately for respiration, which releases energy for growth. Some is turned into starch for storage, and some is used to make cellulose for cell walls. Sugars are carried to other parts of the plant through the phloem.

Limiting Factors

Factors that affect the rate of photosynthesis include light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature. If any one of these is in short supply, it limits the rate. This is called a limiting factor. For example, on a bright day with very little carbon dioxide, the carbon dioxide is the limiting factor. Very high temperatures can damage the enzymes involved and slow the process down.
