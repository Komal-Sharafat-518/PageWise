Newton's Laws of Motion

Sir Isaac Newton described three laws that explain how objects move. They form the base of classical mechanics.

First Law (Law of Inertia). An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed, unless an unbalanced force acts on it. Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its motion. A heavier object has more inertia. For example, passengers lurch forward when a bus brakes suddenly because their bodies tend to keep moving.

Second Law (Force and Acceleration). The acceleration of an object depends on the net force acting on it and on its mass. The formula is F = m x a, where F is force in newtons, m is mass in kilograms and a is acceleration in metres per second squared. If the same force pushes a light cart and a heavy cart, the light cart accelerates more. A force of 10 N on a 2 kg object gives an acceleration of 5 m/s squared.

Third Law (Action and Reaction). For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. When you push against a wall, the wall pushes back on you with the same force. A rocket moves upward because it pushes hot gas downward, and the gas pushes the rocket up. The two forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel each other.

Friction is a force that opposes motion between two surfaces in contact. It is the reason a rolling ball eventually stops. Without friction, the first law says the ball would roll forever.
